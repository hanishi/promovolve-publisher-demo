Western Towns Begin the Long Climb Back After a Record Wildfire Season

As crews finish clearing the last burned acres, residents of the Carverton foothills are sifting through ash and weighing whether to rebuild, even as officials warn the next dangerous season is only months away.

By Diane Okafor — May 24, 2026

The smoke has finally lifted over Pinedale Crossing, and for the first time in nearly four months the residents of this mountain hamlet can see the ridgeline that frames their valley. What they see is not reassuring: a charred slope where a dense pine forest stood last spring, and the blackened foundations of homes that did not survive the worst fire season the region has ever recorded.

By the official count, the fires that swept through the Carverton Range scorched more than 1.4 million acres, destroyed roughly 2,300 structures and forced the temporary evacuation of nine towns. State emergency officials say the season was the most destructive on record by nearly every measure, surpassing even the catastrophic stretch of two summers ago.

Now comes the slower, less visible work of recovery. Insurance adjusters move from lot to lot with clipboards and drones. Contractors are booked into next year. And families who fled with little more than photo albums and pets are confronting decisions about whether to return at all.

"You don't rebuild a town in a season," said Mayor Ruth Calloway of Pinedale Crossing, who has spent her mornings coordinating debris removal and her afternoons fielding calls from anxious homeowners. "You rebuild it one permit, one driveway, one family at a time, and you hope the mountain gives you enough quiet years to finish."

The financial strain is already evident. Several insurers have signaled that they will not renew policies in the highest-risk corridors, leaving homeowners to seek coverage through a state-backed pool that charges steep premiums. Local officials worry that without affordable insurance, longtime residents will be priced out and replaced by seasonal owners who treat the area as a vacation destination.

State foresters, meanwhile, are pressing communities to rethink how they rebuild. New guidance encourages fire-resistant roofing, wider defensible space around structures and a controversial proposal to thin the surrounding woodlands through prescribed burns. Some residents welcome the measures; others see them as an intrusion on land their families have held for generations.

The clock is unforgiving. Climatologists tracking the region note that snowpack came in below average again this winter, and the long-range outlook calls for a hot, dry summer. Crews that spent the spring clearing dead timber are already shifting their attention to cutting firebreaks before the heat returns.

For all the uncertainty, there are signs of resolve. On a recent weekend, dozens of volunteers gathered at the edge of town to replant native seedlings along a creek bed, a small gesture against an enormous loss. "We're not leaving," one resident said, pressing a sapling into the scorched soil. "This is home, and home is worth the climb."
